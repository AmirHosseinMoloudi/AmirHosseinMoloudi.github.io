User experience
12 October 2026

Your Session Expired.
So Did 15 Minutes of Your Life.

Great software isn’t just about performance and architecture. It’s about protecting users’ time when real life interrupts.

One of the most frustrating messages you can encounter online is: “Your session has expired.” Especially when the 15 minutes you spent filling out a form have apparently expired along with it.

You’re almost finished. Your phone rings. You step away for a couple of minutes, come back, and discover that everything you entered is gone.

From the system’s perspective, a session simply expired. From the user’s perspective, 15 minutes of their life just disappeared.

We spend considerable time on performance, architecture, interface design, optimization, and countless technical details. But people don’t use our products under perfect laboratory conditions. Connections drop, tabs close accidentally, attention shifts, and buttons get clicked twice.

A well-engineered product should account for those everyday realities. A lost connection shouldn’t automatically mean re-entering everything. A frozen submission shouldn’t leave someone guessing whether their request was processed. Non-sensitive input should, where appropriate, survive navigation and interruption.

None of this is trivial. Data security, state management, duplicate requests, and recovery all need deliberate engineering.

But those complexities are our responsibility, not the user’s burden.

The purpose of automation is to simplify processes. Users shouldn’t need to understand what happens behind a button, and they shouldn’t have to pay for its technical shortcomings.

In the age of AI, these decisions are an important part of what makes our work valuable: respecting people’s time, attention, and trust.

Reliability / 15 Sep 2026

Retries are not a resilience strategy on their own.

My post explores how retries can amplify an already stressed dependency, particularly when multiple layers retry independently. It discusses bounded timeouts, backoff, jitter, retry limits, safe repetition, circuit breaking, backpressure, and failing fast when appropriate.

Not every error deserves the same response. Sometimes the most reliable decision is to stop retrying and let the dependency recover.
